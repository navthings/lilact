\begin{abstract}
Most transformer MLPs still use ReLU, GELU or SiLU, even though the three perform almost identically and are beaten by both gated MLPs like SwiGLU and by squared ReLU.
SwiGLU pays for its gain with a third weight matrix.
I test whether the same gains are available without it, using four new activations: Group Gate, Rank-ReLU$^2$, Smooth-back ReLU$^2$ and Cross-Square.
Each is compared with ReLU, GELU, SiLU, squared ReLU and SwiGLU in small Llama-style models trained on TinyStories on a MacBook, at matched parameter counts, with a learning-rate sweep per activation and three seeds.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The activation function is the only nonlinear step in a transformer's MLP block.
It is also one of the least tested choices in a typical model: most people copy whatever Llama uses, and my own earlier models used SwiGLU for exactly that reason.

The three classic options barely differ.
In \citet{shazeer2020glu}'s comparison, ReLU \citep{nair2010relu}, GELU \citep{hendrycks2016gelu} and SiLU \citep{elfwing2018silu,ramachandran2017swish} finished at log-perplexities of 1.677, 1.679 and 1.683.
What did make a difference in that study was multiplying two projections of the input together.
A bilinear layer, which does only that and has no nonlinearity at all, beat all three, and SwiGLU did better still.
So the part of the MLP that bends seems to matter less than the part that multiplies.

Squaring is the other change that keeps winning.
Squared ReLU was the most useful single change found by the Primer search \citep{so2021primer}, matched SwiGLU at 1.3B parameters \citep{zhang2024relu2}, and learned activations with an $x^2$ term have beaten SwiGLU at 1B to 3B \citep{huang2024xielu,zhuo2024polycom}.

The catch with SwiGLU is cost.
Its gate needs a whole extra weight matrix, so to keep the parameter count fixed the hidden layer has to shrink from $4d$ to about $\tfrac{8}{3}d$.
This paper asks a narrow question: can an activation get the benefit of gating, squaring or both while keeping the plain two-matrix MLP at full width?

I propose four activations that try (Section~\ref{sec:method}) and test them against five baselines under the same budget (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Gated MLPs.}
Gated linear units multiply two linear projections of the input, one of them passed through a nonlinearity \citep{dauphin2017glu}.
\citet{shazeer2020glu} found that every gated variant they tried beat ReLU, GELU and Swish in T5, with the hidden width cut from 3072 to 2048 to pay for the third matrix.
When \citet{narang2021transfer} later re-tested dozens of published transformer changes in one codebase, most gains vanished, but the GLU variants held up.
\citet{lyu2026glu} give an optimisation explanation: a GLU network's neural tangent kernel is roughly the plain network's kernel multiplied elementwise by the data Gram matrix, which improves its conditioning.

Two existing designs are close to what I do here.
Masked GLU uses one weight matrix for both gate and value and splits it with learned binary masks \citep{tajima2025mglu}.
RWKV's channel-mixing block computes $\sigma(r) \odot W_v \max(k,0)^2$, so it already combines a sigmoid gate with a squared value, but $r$ and $k$ come from separate matrices applied to token-shifted inputs \citep{peng2023rwkv}.
Group Gate and Cross-Square instead take the gate from the same projection as the value.

\paragraph{Squared activations.}
Besides Primer and the ReLU$^2$ study above, PolyCom learns a weighted sum of powers of ReLU or of normalised powers of the input \citep{zhuo2024polycom}, and xIELU adds a learned $x^2$ term on the positive side with an ELU-shaped negative side \citep{huang2024xielu}.
Both beat SwiGLU at 1B scale or above.

\paragraph{Gradient for negative inputs.}
A ReLU unit whose input is negative on every example gets no gradient and stops learning.
xATLU widens the range of its gate so that negative inputs still pass gradient, and credits much of its gain over GELU to that \citep{huang2024xatlu}.
Swapping a flat or non-differentiable backward pass for a smooth stand-in is routine in quantised networks \citep{bengio2013ste} and spiking networks \citep{neftci2019surrogate}.
SUGAR does this for plain ReLU and reports large gains on CIFAR-100 with VGG-16 and ResNet-18 \citep{horuz2025sugar}, but it was only tested on vision models.

\paragraph{Units that depend on each other.}
Spark Transformer keeps roughly the top-$k$ FFN units per token, using a threshold estimated from the mean and standard deviation of the pre-activations; it keeps 8\% of units active at about the same quality as Gemma-2, and its goal is speed \citep{you2025spark}.
SoLU, $x \cdot \mathrm{softmax}(x)$, also makes units compete, for interpretability, and needs a LayerNorm after it to match GELU \citep{elhage2022solu}.

\paragraph{What I leave out.}
Short causal convolutions inside the MLP (Canon-D) help reasoning in synthetic tasks \citep{allenzhu2025canon}, and RWKV mixes in the previous token too.
Every function in this paper looks at one token only, so any difference comes from the activation and not from extra token mixing.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

For one token with hidden state $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, the standard MLP block is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mlp}
  \mathrm{MLP}(x) = W_{\text{down}}\, f(h), \qquad h = W_{\text{up}}\, x, \qquad W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{ff} \times d},
\end{equation}
with $d_{ff} = 4d$.
SwiGLU uses $W_{\text{down}}\big(\mathrm{SiLU}(W x) \odot V x\big)$ with $d_{ff} \approx \tfrac{8}{3}d$, so its three matrices hold as many parameters as two at $4d$ \citep{shazeer2020glu}.
All four new activations keep the two-matrix form at $d_{ff} = 4d$.
$\sigma$ is the sigmoid and $\odot$ is elementwise multiplication.

\subsection{Group Gate}

Split the $d_{ff}$ hidden units into groups of $g$, and give each group its own learned $g \times g$ matrix.
Stacked, these form a block-diagonal matrix $A$.
Each unit's gate is a learned mix of the units in its group:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:groupgate}
  f(h)_i = h_i \cdot \sigma\Big(\sum_{j \in G(i)} A_{ij}\, h_j\Big), \qquad A_{\text{init}} = I.
\end{equation}
At initialisation every unit only looks at itself, so Group Gate starts out as exactly SiLU and training decides how much the units should listen to each other.

Since $h = W_{\text{up}} x$, the gate's input is $(A W_{\text{up}})\, x$.
That makes Group Gate a gated MLP whose gate matrix is forced to be $A W_{\text{up}}$ rather than a free third matrix.
With $g = 2$ and $A$ a swap matrix, each unit is gated by one partner; a larger $g$ lets the gate look in more directions.
The cost is $d_{ff}\, g$ extra parameters per layer and about $2\, d_{ff}\, g$ extra FLOPs per token, a fraction $g/(2d)$ of the block's matrix multiplies, or about 1\% at $d = 384$, $g = 8$.
$A$ is stored as a $(d_{ff}/g, g, g)$ tensor and applied with one batched matrix multiply.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Rank-ReLU$^2$}{Rank-ReLU2}}

Squared ReLU switches a unit on whenever its input is above zero, so how many units are active depends on the scale of the layer and drifts during training.
Rank-ReLU$^2$ switches a unit on only when it stands out from the other units for the same token.
Let $\mu$ be the mean of the token's $d_{ff}$ pre-activations and $s = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(h) + \epsilon}$ their standard deviation, with $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$.
Then
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rank}
  f(h_i) = \frac{\max\big(0,\; h_i - \mu - \tau s\big)^2}{s},
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is learned, one per layer, starting at 0.
If the pre-activations are roughly Gaussian, the share of active units is $1 - \Phi(\tau)$: about 50\% at $\tau = 0$, 16\% at $\tau = 1$ and 7\% at $\tau = 1.5$.
Dividing by $s$ means that for $c > 0$, $f(c\,h) = c\, f(h)$, so the output grows linearly with the input's scale instead of quadratically as squared ReLU does.
I also test a version that stops the gradient through $\mu$ and $s$.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Smooth-back ReLU$^2$}{Smooth-back ReLU2}}

The forward pass is plain squared ReLU, $y = \max(0,x)^2$.
Its true derivative, $2\max(0,x)$, is zero for every negative input, so a unit that goes negative on everything never recovers.
In the backward pass I replace it with the derivative of a smooth lookalike, $\mathrm{softplus}_\beta(x)^2$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:smooth}
  \frac{\partial y}{\partial x} := 2\, \mathrm{softplus}_\beta(x)\, \sigma(\beta x), \qquad \mathrm{softplus}_\beta(x) = \tfrac{1}{\beta} \ln\big(1 + e^{\beta x}\big).
\end{equation}
With $\beta = 1$, an input of $-1$ now gets a gradient of 0.17 instead of 0, and an input of 2 gets 3.75 instead of the true 4.
As $\beta \to \infty$ the true derivative comes back.
The forward pass still produces exact zeros, which is what makes squared ReLU sparse and cheap.
I use $\beta = 1.5$ and test 1 and 4 as ablations.
It is implemented as a custom vector-Jacobian product, about ten lines in MLX.

\subsection{Cross-Square}

Split $h$ into halves $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{ff}/2}$ and pair unit $k$ of $u$ with unit $k$ of $v$.
Each unit is squared and then gated by its partner:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:cross}
  f(h) = \Big[\; \max(0,u)^2 \odot \sigma(v) \;\Big\Vert\; \max(0,v)^2 \odot \sigma(u) \;\Big],
\end{equation}
where $\Vert$ is concatenation, so the output keeps width $d_{ff}$.
This is RWKV's squared-and-gated recipe with the gate taken from the same projection, so it adds no parameters.
The risk is that the two halves learn to copy each other and waste half the layer; Section~\ref{sec:analysis} compares it with an untied version that uses a separate gate matrix.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models.}
Decoder-only Llama-style transformers: pre-norm RMSNorm, rotary position embeddings, no biases, tied input and output embeddings, context length 512.
The small model has $d = 384$, 6 layers and 6 heads (22.9M parameters); the larger has $d = 768$, 12 layers and 12 heads (about 110M).
Only the MLP activation changes between runs, and every model is within about 1\% of the same parameter count (Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}).

\paragraph{Data.}
TinyStories \citep{eldan2023tinystories}, tokenised with the 32k-token Llama tokenizer.
The small model trains on 100M tokens and the larger on 300M.
Training windows are drawn in a shuffled order without replacement, so no token is seen twice.
This matters: a first pilot on Tiny Shakespeare \citep{karpathy2015charrnn} repeated its data about 16 times, the models memorised it, and the activations that fit fastest looked worst (Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}).

\paragraph{Training.}
AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with $\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$, weight decay 0.1 on weight matrices only (never on norms or on the activations' own parameters), gradient clipping at 1.0, linear warmup over the first 2\% of steps and cosine decay to 10\% of the peak learning rate.
Each step sees 16,384 tokens for the small model and 32,768 for the larger, using gradient accumulation.
For every activation I try peak learning rates of $5\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$ and $2\times10^{-3}$ with seed 0, then run seeds 1 and 2 at the best one.
Everything runs in MLX \citep{hannun2023mlx} on a 16GB MacBook Air (M5).
The laptop has no fan and slows down as it heats up, so throughput is compared only between runs started from cold.

\paragraph{What I measure.}
Best and final validation loss (mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds), training throughput, the share of hidden units that never fire on a held-out batch, the share of exact zeros, the largest activation in each layer, and where each activation's learned parameters end up.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This is a laptop-sized study.
The largest model has about 110M parameters, and rankings between activations are known to shift with scale and setup \citep{narang2021transfer}.
There is one dataset, TinyStories, which is simple English and may favour some functions over others.
Only the peak learning rate is tuned, and three seeds can only separate differences larger than about \todo{two standard deviations}.
Throughput numbers come from plain MLX code with no fused kernels, which probably understates how cheap Group Gate and Rank-ReLU$^2$ could be.

\section{Pilot run}
\label{app:pilot}

Before the main experiments I ran a quick pilot: a 4.8M-parameter character-level transformer (6 layers, $d = 256$) on Tiny Shakespeare, 2000 steps of 8,192 characters, two seeds per activation.
It included an early candidate, SiLU plus a learned multiple of its own square, which I later dropped because it belongs to the same family as PolyCom and xIELU.

  \label{tab:pilot}
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Activation & Seed 0 & Seed 1 & Mean \\
    \midrule
    GELU & 1.497 & 1.505 & 1.501 \\
    SiLU + $a \cdot$SiLU$^2$ & 1.501 & 1.505 & 1.503 \\
    ReLU$^2$ & 1.506 & 1.513 & 1.510 \\
    SiLU + $a \cdot$SiLU$^2$, gated & 1.518 & 1.520 & 1.519 \\
    SwiGLU & 1.515 & 1.525 & 1.520 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

The gated models came last, the reverse of the published ranking.
The likely reason is that the 1.1M-character dataset was seen about 16 times, so the models were memorising it, and gating mostly speeds up fitting.
That is why the main experiments use a dataset large enough that nothing repeats.
One thing from the pilot did look real: in both seeds, the learned weight on the squared term grew with depth, from about 0.02 in the first layer to 0.11 in the last.
